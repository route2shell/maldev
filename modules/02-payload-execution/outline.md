
## Outline

|Module|Lesson|Proposed Lesson Title|Short Description|
|---|---|---|---|
|**2A**|**2.1**|**What Is a Payload? Roles, Forms, and Execution Goals**|Defines payloads as executable logic with a mission, distinguishes payload role from delivery mechanism, and introduces the major forms students will work with throughout the course.|
||**2.2**|**EXE vs DLL vs Shellcode: Startup Models and Constraints**|Compares how each payload form begins execution, what runtime assumptions it can rely on, and why each form fits different engineering or operational goals.|
||**2.3**|**Position-Independent Code and Why Shellcode Is Different**|Explains why shellcode must avoid normal assumptions about image base, imports, and relocations, and why PIC is central to memory-resident execution.|
||**2.4**|**Static Imports vs Runtime API Resolution**|Introduces the conceptual difference between PE import tables and runtime lookup patterns so students understand why shellcode must bootstrap its own API access differently.|
||**2.5**|**Anatomy and Lifecycle of a Simple Shellcode Buffer**|Walks through the mental structure of simple shellcode from buffer entrypoint to API resolution to payload logic, emphasizing constraints rather than implementation recipes.|
|**2B**|**2.6**|**From Bytes to Executable Memory: Local Execution Overview**|Establishes the full local-execution mental model: where payload bytes live, how they become executable, and what must happen before control can safely transfer to them.|
||**2.7**|**Allocation and Protection Choices for Runtime Code**|Covers `VirtualAlloc`, protection flags, RW vs RX vs RWX tradeoffs, and why memory-permission changes matter both technically and from a telemetry perspective.|
||**2.8**|**Direct Invocation with Function Pointers**|Explains the function-pointer execution model, why it works for shellcode-like buffers, and what conceptual pitfalls lead to crashes or unstable behavior.|
||**2.9**|**Thread-Based Execution and Start-Routine Semantics**|Introduces `CreateThread`, thread entry expectations, waiting and synchronization concepts, and how thread-based launch differs from direct execution.|
||**2.10**|**Observing Local Execution: Memory, Crashes, and Cleanup**|Consolidates common runtime issues such as bad protections, invalid calling assumptions, cleanup concerns, and what a debugger or process-inspection tool would reveal.|
||**2.11**|**Standard DLL Loading as a Baseline Execution Model**|Uses `LoadLibrary` and `GetProcAddress` as the normal Windows baseline so students can later appreciate how reflective or manual loading differs from the standard path.|
|**2C**|**2.12**|**Where Payload Bytes Live Before Execution**|Introduces payload placement as a separate design problem: sections, heap allocations, resources, local files, and retrieved buffers all represent different storage surfaces.|
||**2.13**|**Embedded Payloads in Sections and Resources**|Explains how payload data can live inside sections or `.rsrc`, what that changes about retrieval and visibility, and why embedded blobs are common in staged designs.|
||**2.14**|**Stagers, Stages, and Delivery Taxonomy**|Defines the stager concept cleanly and separates loader, stager, stage, and final payload so the learner stops conflating storage, transport, and execution.|
||**2.15**|**File-Backed Staging Patterns**|Examines temporary disk-backed staging, dropped intermediates, process-launch patterns, and the tradeoffs of touching the filesystem even briefly.|
||**2.16**|**Memory-Only Retrieval and In-Memory Staging**|Covers the conceptual flow of retrieving payload bytes into memory, preparing them for execution, and keeping them off disk while still accepting that memory remains observable.|
||**2.17**|**What “Fileless” Means — and What It Does Not**|Tightens the overloaded “fileless” term into a precise teaching taxonomy so students distinguish memory-only execution, no-final-file payloads, registry-backed storage, and remote retrieval patterns.|
||**2.18**|**Delivery Surface Comparison: Disk, Memory, Resources, and Network**|Synthesizes the staging module by comparing where artifacts appear, what defenders can inspect, and how different delivery choices affect complexity and observability.|
|**2D**|**2.19**|**Encoding vs Encryption vs Obfuscation vs Packing**|Establishes a clean taxonomy so students stop treating all payload-transformation methods as equivalent and instead understand each method’s purpose and limitations.|
||**2.20**|**XOR and Lightweight Decode Stubs**|Uses XOR as the simplest transformation model to teach key ideas about stored-vs-decoded bytes, runtime decode steps, and why simple methods still appear in real tooling.|
||**2.21**|**Base64, BaseN, and Transport-Friendly Representations**|Explains when encoding is used instead of encryption, how it changes payload representation, and why representation tricks are useful but not secretly “crypto.”|
||**2.22**|**Real Cryptography for Payload Protection**|Introduces stronger in-memory protection concepts with a stream-cipher example such as ChaCha20, focusing on keys, nonces, runtime decryption, and engineering tradeoffs.|
||**2.23**|**Configuration Blobs, Embedded Settings, and Key Material**|Covers how loaders or implants carry configuration, timing, URLs, flags, and crypto material, and why config storage is its own design problem separate from payload bytes.|
||**2.24**|**What Transformation Changes — and What It Cannot Hide**|Connects encoding/encryption/obfuscation back to defender visibility by showing that transformed payloads must still exist decoded or decrypted somewhere during execution.|
||**2.25**|**Payload Safety, Sandboxing, and Instrumented Lab Workflow**|Closes the module with safe research workflow, snapshots, isolation, and process/memory observation practices so students treat payload experimentation as an instrumented learning exe|
